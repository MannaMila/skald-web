Which Odyssey translation should I read?

The short answer: for a first read in contemporary English, start with Emily Wilson (2017) — line-for-line iambic pentameter, plain syntax, fast and clear — or Robert Fagles (1996), the driving performance verse that put Homer on a generation of syllabuses. Robert Fitzgerald (1961) is the lyric classic; Richmond Lattimore (1965) stays closest to the Greek line. And if you want to start reading in the next five minutes for free, Samuel Butler's plain prose (1900) and George Herbert Palmer's read-aloud prose (1884–91) are both in the public domain. Chapman (1615) and Pope (1725–26) are free too, but they are period masterpieces — read them second.

The longer answer is that there is no single best translation — there are voices, and the right one depends on what you want. The atlas charts 233 translations, into 50+ languages, across 2,300 years; below are the openings of seventeen of them, so you can hear the difference before you commit.

Hear the difference: seventeen openings of the same poem

Every translator answers the same opening line: Homer asks the Muse for the story of a single adjective — polytropos, the man "of many turns." What each one does with it tells you what the next twelve thousand lines will feel like. Homer

Ancient Greek · c. 8th century BCE · dactylic hexameter
ἄνδρα μοι ἔννεπε, μοῦσα, πολύτροπον, ὃς μάλα πολλὰ
πλάγχθη, ἐπεὶ Τροίης ἱερὸν πτολίεθρον ἔπερσεν·

Homer, Odyssey, Od. 1.1–2 (text: Perseus) — literally, “Tell me, Muse, of the man of many turns, who wandered far after he sacked Troy’s sacred citadel.”

George Chapman

1614–15 · rhymed iambic-pentameter couplets
The man, O Muse, inform, that many a way
Wound with his wisdom to his wished stay;
That wander’d wondrous far, when he the town
Of sacred Troy had sack’d and shiver’d down;

George Chapman, Homer’s Odysses, 1614–15, Od. 1.1–4

The first complete Odyssey in English — the book that kept John Keats up till dawn one October night in 1816 and yielded "On First Looking into Chapman's Homer" by morning.

Atlas record · Read free (Project Gutenberg)

Thomas Hobbes

1675 · rhymed decasyllabic couplets
Tell me, O Muse, th’ adventures of the man
That having sack’d the sacred town of Troy,
Wander’d so long at sea; what course he ran
By winds and tempests driven from his way:

Thomas Hobbes, The Odysses of Homer, 1675, Od. 1.1–4 (text from Molesworth’s English Works vol. X)

The philosopher of Leviathan spent his final decade on Homer, publishing a complete Odyssey in 1675 as "the amusement of his old age" — plain, brisk, story-first verse.

Atlas record · Read free (Online Library of Liberty)

Alexander Pope

1725–26 · heroic couplets · with Broome & Fenton
The man for wisdom’s various arts renown’d,
Long exercised in woes, O Muse! resound;
Who, when his arms had wrought the destined fall
Of sacred Troy, and razed her heaven-built wall,

Alexander Pope (with William Broome and Elijah Fenton), The Odyssey of Homer, 1725–26, Od. 1.1–4

A workshop production behind a single glittering style — Broome englished eight books, Fenton four, Pope twelve — that fixed the sound of Homer for the English eighteenth century.

Atlas record · Read free (Project Gutenberg)

William Cowper

1791 · blank verse
Muse make the man thy theme, for shrewdness famed
And genius versatile, who far and wide
A Wand’rer, after Ilium overthrown,
Discover’d various cities, and the mind
And manners learn’d of men, in lands remote.

William Cowper, The Odyssey of Homer, 1791, Od. 1.1–5

Conceived as the antidote to Pope: blank verse instead of rhyme, closeness instead of couplet glitter. Every later blank-verse Odyssey works in the space Cowper opened.

Atlas record · Read free (Project Gutenberg)

William Cullen Bryant

1871 · blank verse
Tell me, O Muse, of that sagacious man
Who, having overthrown the sacred town
Of Ilium, wandered far and visited
The capitals of many nations, learned
The customs of their dwellers…

William Cullen Bryant, The Odyssey of Homer, 1871, Od. 1.1–5 (text: Osgood printing, 1873)

America's senior poet, in his late seventies: grave, plain, unhurried — the major American poetic Odyssey of the nineteenth century.

Atlas record · Read free (Internet Archive, vol. I)

S. H. Butcher & Andrew Lang

1879 · archaizing literary prose
Tell me, Muse, of that man, so ready at need, who wandered far and wide, after he had sacked the sacred citadel of Troy, and many were the men whose towns he saw and whose mind he learnt…

S. H. Butcher and Andrew Lang, The Odyssey of Homer Done into English Prose, 1879, Od. 1.1–3

The Victorian standard, cast in deliberately King James-flavored English — for a generation this was simply how Homer sounded.

Atlas record · Read free (Project Gutenberg)

George Herbert Palmer

1884–91 · literary prose
Speak to me, Muse, of the adventurous man who wandered long after he sacked the sacred citadel of Troy. Many the men whose towns he saw, whose ways he proved…

George Herbert Palmer, The Odyssey of Homer, 1884/1891, Od. 1.1–3

The Harvard philosopher published Books I–XII in 1884 and the complete poem in 1891, in supple, faintly rhythmic prose that reads well aloud. Houghton Mifflin kept it before generations of students — one of the most widely read American prose Odysseys.

Atlas record · Read free (Internet Archive)

William Morris

1887 · rhymed accentual long lines
Tell me, O Muse, of the Shifty, the man who wandered afar,
After the Holy Burg, Troy-town, he had wasted with war;
He saw the towns of menfolk, and the mind of men did he learn;
As he warded his life in the world, and his fellow-farers' return,

William Morris, The Odyssey of Homer Done into English Verse, 1887, Od. 1.1–5

Homer as northern romance, thick with saga-flavored diction. Reviewing it in 1887, Oscar Wilde called it "a true work of art, a rendering not merely of language into language, but of poetry into poetry."

Atlas record · Read free (Internet Archive, vol. I)

Samuel Butler

1900 · plain prose
Tell me, O Muse, of that ingenious hero who travelled far and wide after he had sacked the famous town of Troy.

Samuel Butler, The Odyssey Rendered into English Prose, 1900, Od. 1.1–2

Butler wanted an Odyssey that reads like a novel — the plainest English version yet, written alongside his cheerfully heretical case that a young Sicilian woman composed the poem. Still one of the easiest Odysseys to find and read free online.

Atlas record · Read free (Project Gutenberg)

A. T. Murray

1919 · literal Loeb prose
Tell me, O Muse, of the man of many devices, who wandered full many ways after he had sacked the sacred citadel of Troy.

A. T. Murray, The Odyssey (Loeb Classical Library), 1919, Od. 1.1–2

The twentieth century's standard scholarly crib: Greek on the left page, "a fairly literal translation" facing it. It still underpins the freely readable Odyssey on Perseus.

T. E. Lawrence

1932 · stylized prose
O Divine Poesy, Goddess-daughter of Zeus, sustain for me this song of the various-minded man.

T. E. Lawrence (T. E. Shaw), The Odyssey of Homer, 1932, Od. 1.1–2

Lawrence of Arabia — then serving in the RAF as T. E. Shaw — translated Homer around service duties; the 1932 edition is counted among the most beautiful printed books of the century.

E. V. Rieu

1945/46 · accessible prose
The hero of the tale which I beg the Muse to help me tell is that resourceful man who roamed the wide world after he had sacked the holy citadel of Troy.

E. V. Rieu, The Odyssey (Penguin Classics), 1946, Od. 1.1–2

The first Penguin Classic ever published, on the gamble that ordinary readers wanted Homer in unfussy modern prose. It sold about three million copies — the paperback that taught postwar Britain to read the classics.

Atlas record

Robert Fitzgerald

1961 · free verse
Sing in me, Muse, and through me tell the story
of that man skilled in all ways of contending,

Robert Fitzgerald, The Odyssey, 1961, Od. 1.1–2

Homer as a modern American poem in its own right — supple, musical lines a generation knew by heart. The lyric pole of the great mid-century pair, opposite Lattimore.

Atlas record

Richmond Lattimore

1965 · six-beat line
Tell me, Muse, of the man of many ways, who was driven
far journeys, after he had sacked Troy's sacred citadel.

Richmond Lattimore, The Odyssey of Homer, 1965, Od. 1.1–2

The scholar's counterweight: line by line, epithets and formulas intact — the version to read when you want to know, as nearly as English allows, what the Greek actually says.

Atlas record

Robert Fagles

1996 · irregular verse
Sing to me of the man, Muse, the man of twists and turns
driven time and again off course, once he had plundered

Robert Fagles, The Odyssey, 1996, Od. 1.1–2

The blockbuster Odyssey of the late twentieth century: a performance text built to be read aloud, over a million copies sold, the version that put Homer onto American high-school syllabuses.

Atlas record

Ian Johnston

2002/2006 · blank verse · public domain since 2024
Muse, speak to me now of that resourceful man
who wandered far and wide after ravaging
the sacred citadel of Troy. He came to see
many people's cities, where he learned their customs,

Ian Johnston, The Odyssey, online 2002 / book 2006, Od. 1.1–4 (public domain since January 2024)

Plain-spoken modern verse, paced for reading aloud — and in January 2024 Johnston released it into the public domain, free to download, share, and adapt.

Atlas record · Read free (archived text)

Emily Wilson

2017 · iambic pentameter, line-for-line
Tell me about a complicated man.
Muse, tell me how he wandered and was lost

Emily Wilson, The Odyssey, 2017, Od. 1.1–2

The first complete published English Odyssey by a woman: 12,110 lines of iambic pentameter matching her chosen Greek text line for line, opening with a quiet shock that reset the public argument over what a contemporary Odyssey should sound like.

Common questions

What's the best Odyssey translation for a first-time reader?

Wilson (2017) or Fagles (1996). Wilson's line-for-line iambic pentameter and plain syntax read fast and clean in contemporary English; Fagles's driving, irregular verse was built to be read aloud and put Homer onto American high-school syllabuses. Fitzgerald (1961) is the lyric classic of the previous generation. If you would rather ease in with prose, Butler (1900) is the plainest, and it is free. The honest answer is the one this page is built around: read the openings above and pick the voice you want in your head for twelve thousand lines.

Which Odyssey translation is closest to the Greek?

Lattimore (1965). He renders Homer line by line in a loose six-beat rhythm and keeps the repeated epithets and formulas of the oral poetry — the version to read when you want to know, as nearly as English allows, what the Greek actually says. A. T. Murray's 1919 Loeb prose is the literal scholarly crib, and it is free to read online. Wilson's 12,110 lines match her chosen Greek text line for line, in contemporary idiom.

Which Odyssey translations are free to read?

Everything on this page published in 1930 or earlier is public domain in the United States: Chapman (1614–15), Hobbes (1675), Pope (1725–26), Cowper (1791), Bryant (1871), Butcher & Lang (1879), Palmer (1884–91), Morris (1887), Butler (1900) and Murray (1919). Butler and Butcher & Lang are one click away on Project Gutenberg; Bryant, Palmer, Morris and Murray are on the Internet Archive. Ian Johnston released his modern verse translation into the public domain in January 2024. The atlas has a "Free to read" filter that shows which translations carry a link to a free text.

Should I read the Odyssey in verse or prose?

Verse keeps the fact that the Odyssey is a poem — rhythm, momentum, formulaic repetition. Prose reads like a novel and tends to be faster for story-first reading. Samuel Butler produced his 1900 prose precisely because he wanted an Odyssey that reads like a novel, and Penguin launched its Classics list in January 1946 on E. V. Rieu's prose on the same bet — it sold around three million copies. If you are torn, Wilson gives you the poem without the archaism.

Is there a good Odyssey audiobook?

Samuel Butler's 1900 prose is free on Project Gutenberg in text and in audio — the easiest Odyssey to start listening to at no cost. Ian Johnston's verse translation, public domain since January 2024, was issued as a Naxos audiobook in 2007. This guide rates translations, not recordings: pick the voice first from the openings above, then find it read aloud.

How many times has the Odyssey been translated?

The Skald Odyssey Translation Atlas records 233 translations, into 50+ languages, across 2,300 years — every one of them an answer to the same opening line.

Who first translated the Odyssey, and who was the first woman to publish one in English?

Livius Andronicus made the oldest known translation — the Latin Odusia, late in the third century BCE, of which about 46 fragmentary lines survive. Anne Dacier's learned French prose Odyssey of 1716 became the standard French Homer of the eighteenth century. Emily Wilson's 2017 version is the first complete published English Odyssey by a woman.
